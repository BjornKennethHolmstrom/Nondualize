# Space: The Boundless Here

> Where exactly are the boundaries of "here" and "there"?

*You are here. The world is there. Inside and outside, near and far, self and other—spatial separation seems as obvious as time's flow. You occupy a specific location in space, bounded by skin, contained in a body, separate from everything else. This sense of being "in here" looking out at a world "out there" is so fundamental that questioning it feels absurd. And yet... when you actually look for the boundary between inside and outside, can you find it? Where exactly does "you" end and "world" begin? This article examines space not as physicists measure it, but as you directly experience it. What if the separation between here and there is conceptual division imposed on boundless awareness rather than actual metaphysical fact?*

## The Conventional View

Let's begin with how most of us experience space most of the time.

**Space separates things**. You are here, the chair is there, other people are over there. Distance creates separation. Objects occupy distinct locations. The universe is a container with things arranged in it at various positions.

**You are inside your body**. Your consciousness is located somewhere in your head, probably behind your eyes or in your brain. You look out at the world from this internal position. There's a clear boundary—skin—that marks where "you" ends and "not-you" begins.

**Space has geometry**. Things are near or far, left or right, up or down. You navigate through space by moving your body from one location to another. Space is the medium through which you travel, the distance you must cross to reach what's separate from you.

**Inside and outside are real**. Your thoughts and feelings are inside. Objects and other people are outside. Private and public, inner and outer, subjective and objective—these divisions map onto spatial structure. What's inside your skin is you; what's outside is world.

This view organizes almost everything. Architecture, personal boundaries, property, privacy, journey, distance, connection—all depend on space as separator, container, measurer of what's here versus there.

And practically, this works. You don't walk through walls. You can't be in two locations simultaneously. Distance matters for communication, relationship, survival.

But is spatial separation as you experience it the same as spatial separation in reality? Or is it interpretive framework, useful map, conceptual overlay on something more primary?

## The Direct Inquiry

Let's examine your actual experience rather than assumptions about experience.

### Where Is the Boundary?

Right now, can you find the edge between "inside" and "outside"?

You might point to your skin. "Here's the boundary—inside the skin is me, outside is world."

But look more carefully. Is skin actually a boundary or just another sensation? Touch your arm. The sensation of touching happens—but where? Is it inside or outside? The sensation isn't on one side of skin looking at the other side. It's just sensation, appearing in awareness.

Now notice sounds. Are they inside or outside? You might say "outside—the sound comes from the external world." But you don't actually hear sounds "out there." You hear them in awareness, just like you see colors in awareness, just like you feel sensations in awareness.

Where is awareness? Inside your head? Then how does it contain the entire visual field, which appears to be "out there"? If awareness is inside, how does the outside get in?

Try to find the actual boundary. Not the concept of boundary, but the experiential edge where "in here" definitively ends and "out there" definitively begins.

### Where Are You Located?

Close your eyes. Where are you?

You might say "I'm in my body." But where in your body? Your head? Your chest? Your whole body?

Notice that wherever you place yourself, you're aware of that location. You're aware of head, aware of chest, aware of body. If you're aware of them, aren't you the awareness rather than those locations?

So where is awareness located? In the brain? Then who's aware of the brain? If awareness is in the brain, how are you aware of your feet—does awareness travel down there? If it's everywhere in the body, is it the body or something that includes the body?

Open your eyes. Now where are you? Still "in here" looking out? But the entire visual field—walls, ceiling, objects—appears in awareness just as directly as bodily sensations appear in awareness. Are you inside the visual field or is the visual field inside you?

Try to locate yourself precisely. Not conceptually—"I'm in my head because that's where the brain is." Experientially—where is the actual center of your experience? Can you find it?

### What Contains What?

The conventional view says: space contains you. You're in the room, the room is in the building, the building is on Earth, Earth is in space.

But in direct experience, isn't it reversed?

The room appears in your awareness. The sense of being "in" the room is concept arising in awareness. The visual field, sounds, sensations—all appearing in awareness, not awareness appearing in them.

Which contains which? Does space contain awareness, or does awareness contain space?

You might object: "Obviously physical space exists independent of my awareness. When I leave the room, it's still there."

But notice what you're doing. You're imagining the room continuing to exist when you're not aware of it. That imagining is happening in your awareness now. You've never actually experienced a room that awareness wasn't aware of—that's contradiction in terms.

This doesn't mean rooms pop into existence when you enter them. It means you've never verified that space exists independent of awareness. Your only access to space is through awareness. The relationship between space-as-you-experience-it and space-as-it-might-be-without-awareness remains mysterious.

### The Boundary Question Intensified

Let's push this further. Take another person—someone sitting near you or someone you know well.

Where are they? "Over there," you say, pointing to a location separate from you.

But how do you know they're over there? Through perception—seeing them, hearing them, perhaps feeling them if you touch. All of this perception happens in awareness.

Do you ever actually experience the other person "out there," or do you experience perceptions of them appearing "in here" in awareness? The visual appearance of them, the sound of their voice, the feeling of their hand—all of this is directly experienced in awareness, not at some removed location called "there."

This isn't solipsism—the claim that only you exist. It's more radical than that. It's the recognition that the spatial division between self and other might be conceptual structure rather than experiential reality.

The other person is real. Their experience is as valid as yours. But the "thereness" of them, the spatial separation between you and them—is that experientially verified or conceptually assumed?

### Where Does Seeing Happen?

Look at something across the room. A lamp, a window, a wall.

Where does the seeing happen? Does it happen at your eyes, which then send information to your brain? Does it happen in your brain, constructing an image from nerve signals? Or does it happen "out there" where the object is?

None of these explanations match direct experience. In direct experience, there's just seeing. Not seeing happening "in here" of something "out there." Just the appearance of the lamp, the window, the wall—arising in awareness that has no location.

The scientific explanation—light reflects off objects, enters eyes, gets processed by brain, creates experience—is useful model. But it's not your direct experience. Your direct experience is: appearance happens. The lamp appears. The wall appears. Where? In boundless awareness that's neither here nor there.

The moment you try to locate the seeing, you create a conceptual framework: viewer here, viewed there, seeing bridging them. But prior to that framework, there's just seamless appearing.

Can you notice that? Not as new understanding to believe, but as direct recognition of what's already happening?

## The Recognition

When you look closely enough, repeatedly enough, honestly enough, something becomes clear:

**Boundaries are conceptual**. You can think "inside and outside," you can feel sensations that get labeled as "at the boundary," but you can't actually find an experiential edge where awareness definitively ends. Skin is sensation appearing in awareness, not edge of awareness.

**Location is relative**. "Here" only means something in relation to "there." But both "here" and "there" are concepts arising in awareness that itself has no location. Space is relationship between perceptions, not container that perceptions exist in.

**Distance is inference**. You don't directly experience the distance between you and that tree. You experience a visual appearance (the tree looks small), a kinesthetic memory (it would take time to walk there), conceptual knowledge (it's fifty feet away). All of these are present experiences. The actual distance is abstraction.

**Awareness is not localized**. It's not in your head or in your body or in any location. Locations appear in awareness. The head, the body, the room, the universe—all appearing in awareness that has no position in space.

You might notice that attention moves—it shifts from the screen to a sound to a bodily feeling. But this movement happens within the field of awareness. Awareness itself is the unchanging, boundless background that contains both what is in the foreground of attention and what is in the background. The spotlight of attention moves across the stage, but the stage itself—awareness—doesn't move. You are the stage, not the spotlight.

**The separation between self and world is constructed**. There is experiencing, and there is conceptual division of that experiencing into experiencer (in here) and experienced (out there). But in direct experience, there's just the experiencing itself—seamless, boundless, without inherent division.

This isn't philosophy. It's description of what's actually happening when you look without the filter of spatial concepts.

## But What About...?

The usual questions and objections arise. Let's address them directly.

### Don't Distances Actually Exist?

Yes, in a relative sense. If you walk toward the tree, the visual appearance changes in predictable ways. If you reach for a cup, your hand travels a measurable distance. All of this is real as far as it goes.

But notice what you're calling "distance." It's a relationship between perceptions—how the tree looks from here versus there, how long it takes your hand to reach the cup. The distance itself isn't directly perceived. It's inferred from changes in perception.

This doesn't make distance unreal or unusable. It makes it relational rather than absolute. A useful measurement, not an inherent property of reality.

And most crucially: the distance appears in awareness. You don't cross awareness to reach the tree. The tree's appearance changes within awareness. All of it—the close view, the far view, the walking—happens in the same boundless awareness.

### Don't Boundaries Protect You?

Absolutely. You need to know where your body is to avoid walking into walls. You need personal boundaries to maintain healthy relationships. You need to distinguish self from other to function in social reality.

The recognition isn't "boundaries don't exist." It's "boundaries are functional constructs, not metaphysical absolutes." They serve without being ultimately real.

You can honor boundaries while knowing they're not finding actual edges in awareness. You can say "this is my space" while recognizing that ownership is conceptual. You can maintain appropriate distance while seeing that separation is relative rather than absolute.

Recognition doesn't eliminate the need for skillful boundaries. If anything, it allows better boundaries—you're not defending an imagined self against an imagined threat, you're responding appropriately to actual situations.

### What About Other People's Experience?

This question is crucial because it touches on ethics, relationship, empathy.

The recognition that boundaries are conceptual doesn't mean other people don't exist or don't matter. They exist as much as you do—which is to say, they arise in awareness just as the sense of "you" arises in awareness.

Their experience is real. Their perspective is as valid as yours. The fact that you can't find a spatial boundary between self and other doesn't mean their suffering doesn't matter or that their needs are imaginary.

In fact, the opposite tends to happen. When the rigid boundary between self and other softens, empathy increases. You recognize that the separation was never as absolute as it seemed. What you do to other, you do to the whole field in which both self and other appear. Harm to them is harm to the interconnected reality you both are expressions of.

This isn't mushy oneness that ignores real differences. It's recognition that differences arise within unity, not as absolute separation.

### Don't I Need a Sense of Self Here?

For practical living, yes—you need a functional sense of being a located person. When someone calls your name, you respond. When you need to eat, you feed this body not that one. Relative selfhood serves survival, communication, coordination.

The recognition doesn't eliminate functional self. It reveals that the self you defend, worry about, build identity around—that constructed self—is conceptual rather than metaphysical. There's a perspective here (these eyes, not those), but the perspective arises in awareness that has no location.

You can function with a sense of "I'm here" while recognizing that the "here" is provisional, useful, but not ultimately bounded in the way it seems.

Many people report that after this recognition, the sense of self becomes lighter, more flexible, less defended. It's there when needed and relaxes when not needed, rather than being constant rigid structure requiring maintenance.

### What About Physics?

Physics describes space mathematically—three dimensions, spacetime curvature, quantum fields. This is incredibly useful for prediction and technology.

But physics describes relationships between measurements, not what space ultimately is. Einstein showed that space is relative—it curves, dilates, depends on reference frame. There's no absolute space that everything exists in.

Quantum mechanics suggests something even stranger. Entangled particles influence each other regardless of spatial distance. The notion that things are separated in space becomes questionable at fundamental levels.

Some physicists speak of space as emergent—not fundamental to reality but arising from more basic processes. The holographic principle suggests that three-dimensional space might be projection of information encoded on a two-dimensional surface.

This recognition does not deny the consistency and shared nature of the world. The fact that the room appears the same when you return to it points to a stable, lawful manifestation within awareness. The mystery isn't that the world is unstable, but that its very existence, stability, and laws are inseparable from the conscious awareness that knows them. The "physical world" is the name we give to this consistent, shared manifestation in consciousness.

Physics doesn't prove that awareness is more fundamental than space. But it does show that space-as-we-experience-it isn't identical to space-as-it-fundamentally-is. This opens room for the recognition that space might be framework for organizing experience rather than container that experience happens in.

## Living From Boundless Awareness

So what does recognizing boundlessness actually change about living?

### Reduced Claustrophobia and Expansion

When you're identified with being located "in here," there's often a sense of constriction. You're trapped in this body, limited to this perspective, stuck in this location. Life is effort to expand your cramped position, to get out of constraints, to reach toward what's beyond your boundaries.

When you recognize boundlessness, that claustrophobia dissolves. You're not in the body looking out—you're the awareness in which the body appears. The body is in you more than you are in the body.

This doesn't mean you suddenly have mystical powers to be in multiple locations. It means the sense of being limited, cramped, trapped in a small space loosens. You recognize yourself as the open space in which all locations appear.

Paradoxically, this makes being in a body easier, not harder. Less effort to escape what you're not actually trapped in. Less anxiety about being confined to what you're actually not confined to.

### Deepened Intimacy

When the boundary between self and other is recognized as conceptual rather than absolute, intimacy changes quality.

Usually, intimacy is attempt to overcome separation—to bridge the gap between self and other, to reach across distance to touch what's fundamentally apart from you. There's always residual separation, always the sense that complete union is impossible because you're here and they're there.

With recognition, intimacy is remembering what was never actually separate. The boundary softens not because you cross it but because you see it was never as solid as it seemed. The other appears in the same awareness you appear in. Their experience and yours arise in the same field.

This doesn't eliminate the uniqueness of perspectives. You still have your experience and they have theirs. But the absolute separation—the sense that you're fundamentally isolated in separate containers—loosens. Connection becomes more natural, less effortful, less defended.

Some report this as feeling more permeable, more transparent, less boundaried. But healthy boundaries remain—you're just not defending an ultimately non-existent separate self against an ultimately non-separate other.

### Changed Relationship to Environment

When the division between inside and outside softens, your relationship to environment transforms.

Usually, you're in environment—a self contained in skin, moving through external space, impacted by surroundings but fundamentally separate from them. Environment is something you're in, not something you are.

With recognition, the inside/outside division becomes more fluid. Yes, this body has boundaries. But awareness itself doesn't stop at skin. The room, the sky, the trees—all appearing in the same awareness that bodily sensations appear in.

This often brings a sense of being more at home in the world. Less alienated, less foreign, less like a separate thing navigating hostile territory. The world isn't "out there" as much—it's here, in awareness, like everything else.

This can change ecological relationship, aesthetic appreciation, sense of belonging. When boundary between self and world softens, care for the world becomes more natural—it's not separate from you, so harming it is harming what you are.

### Meditation Without Effort

Most meditation instructions involve some version of "be here now" or "rest in spacious awareness" or "let go of thoughts." This assumes you're not already in spacious awareness and need to get there.

When you recognize that awareness is already boundless, already here, already what you are—meditation becomes natural. You're not trying to achieve spaciousness. You're noticing the spaciousness that's already present.

Thoughts can arise without closing the space. Sensations can appear without limiting awareness. The body can have its boundaries without awareness being bounded. Nothing needs to be different for awareness to be what it already is.

This doesn't replace formal practice. But it infuses practice with recognition rather than striving. You're not expanding awareness—that's impossible. You're recognizing that awareness is already boundless and always was.

### Freedom of Movement

Paradoxically, recognizing that you're not located makes moving through locations easier.

When you're identified with being here as opposed to there, every movement is effort to change what you are. You're trying to not be where you are and be where you aren't. This creates resistance, anxiety about being in the wrong place.

When you recognize boundlessness, movement is just appearance changing within awareness. You're not moving from here to there—perceptions are changing in the boundless field you are. The "here" and "there" are relative positions, not absolute locations defining what you are.

This often makes travel lighter, relocation less traumatic, being in unfamiliar places less disorienting. You're always home in awareness, regardless of where the body appears to be.

## Practices for Recognition

These aren't techniques to become boundless—you already are. They're ways to notice what's already true.

### The Boundary Search

Throughout your day, try to find the boundary between inside and outside.

Touch your skin. Is that the boundary? Or is it just another sensation appearing in awareness?

Listen to sounds. Are they outside? Or are they appearing in the same awareness that thoughts appear in?

Look at the visual field. Is it out there? Or is it appearing here in awareness, just as close as bodily sensations?

Now notice sensations you'd typically call "internal"—a hunger pang, a feeling of sadness, the sensation of your heartbeat. Then notice sensations you'd call "external"—the feeling of the chair against your skin, the air on your face, the temperature of the room.

Look closely at the raw data of these sensations. Is there an inherent quality of "internality" or "externality" in the sensation itself? Or are both simply sensations appearing in awareness, with the labels "internal" and "external" being thoughts added afterward?

The feeling of being "in here" is itself a constellation of sensations appearing in awareness—not the location of awareness itself.

Do this search repeatedly, briefly, without trying to achieve any special state. Just notice: can you actually find where "in here" definitively ends and "out there" definitively begins?

### The Location Question

Repeatedly ask: "Where am I?"

Not conceptually—"I'm in my house on Main Street." Experientially—where is the actual center of your experience?

In your head? Then what's aware of your head?
In your body? Then what's aware of your body?
Everywhere? Then what does "where" even mean?

Let the question dissolve the assumption that you have a location. Not as new belief ("I am boundless!") but as recognition that location is concept, not direct experience.

### The Container Reversal

Notice what you usually think contains what.

You think: I'm in my body, my body is in the room, the room is in the building.

Now reverse it: The body appears in awareness. The room appears in awareness. The building appears in awareness.

Which feels more accurate to direct experience? Not according to physics or common sense—according to what's actually happening right now?

Don't believe either version. Just notice which is closer to the immediacy of experiencing itself.

### The Hand Experiment

Hold your hand in front of your face. Look at it.

Where is your hand? You'll probably say "out there, in front of me."

But look more carefully. You're not seeing the hand "out there"—you're seeing a visual appearance arising in awareness. The appearance is as immediately present as any thought or feeling. It's not removed at a distance called "there."

Now touch your hand to your face. Feel the sensation.

Where is the sensation? Inside or outside? Or is it just sensation, appearing in awareness that has no inside or outside?

Move your hand through space. Watch the visual appearance change, feel the kinesthetic sensations.

All of this—the visual changes, the felt movement, the sense of space—appears in awareness. The awareness itself doesn't move. Experiences change within boundless awareness.

Can you notice that directly? Not as idea, but as what's actually happening?

### The Other Person Investigation

When you're with someone, notice your assumptions about where they are.

You probably assume they're "over there"—separate, at a distance, in their own location.

But how do you actually experience them? Through perceptions appearing in awareness. Their visual appearance, their voice, the feeling of their presence—all appearing in the same awareness your thoughts appear in.

Now close your eyes and think of the person. A mental image of them appears. Where is that image? Is it "in your head"? Or is it, like all other images and thoughts, appearing in the space of awareness itself?

Notice that whether your eyes are open (seeing their visual form) or closed (thinking of them), the experience of them is an appearance in your awareness. The sense of a separate, independently existing "other" over there is a compelling story, but the raw experience is always a perception or a thought here, in the boundless here of awareness.

This doesn't mean they don't exist. It means the "thereness" is conceptual. In direct experience, there's just perceptions arising in awareness.

Stay with this when you're in conversation. Notice the perceptions arising. Notice the tendency to conceptualize separation. Notice what's actually happening versus what you assume is happening.

This can profoundly change how you relate. The conceptual distance softens. The absolute separation relaxes. Intimacy becomes more available because the barrier was never as solid as it seemed.

## Integration Challenges

Recognizing boundlessness doesn't instantly transform all habits of spatial thinking. Several challenges typically arise:

### Boundaries Can Feel Violated

If there's no ultimate boundary between self and other, how do you maintain healthy limits? How do you say no? How do you protect yourself?

The recognition isn't "boundaries don't exist." It's "boundaries are functional, not absolute." You still need to maintain appropriate distance, say no to what doesn't serve, protect your wellbeing.

The difference is you're not defending an imaginary separate self. You're responding skillfully to actual situations. Boundaries become more flexible—present when needed, absent when not, always in service of appropriate response rather than rigid defense.

### Spatial Language Persists

You'll continue using spatial language. "I'm here," "you're there," "the cup is on the table." This is fine—language serves communication, not metaphysical description.

The recognition doesn't require abandoning conventional speech. It just means knowing that the spatial framework is convenient fiction rather than ultimate reality. You can speak as if you're located while knowing that location is relative concept arising in boundless awareness.

### The Body Still Needs Care

Recognizing that awareness is boundless doesn't mean the body is invulnerable. You still need to eat, sleep, avoid injury. The body has limits even if awareness doesn't.

This recognition isn't permission to neglect physical needs or test boundaries recklessly. The body is valuable expression of consciousness in form. Care for it while knowing it's appearance in awareness rather than container of awareness.

### Others May Not Understand

If you try to explain this recognition to people who haven't explored it, you'll likely confuse them. "I'm not located" sounds crazy or pretentious or delusional.

This is normal. The recognition is subtle and runs counter to deeply embedded assumptions. Most people aren't questioning spatial separation, so talking about it seems strange.

Better to embody the recognition than explain it. Live with more openness, less defensiveness, greater intimacy. Let the recognition show in how you are rather than what you claim.

## Common Misunderstandings

### "I Am the Universe"

This phrase gets used but often creates more confusion than clarity. It can become spiritual inflation—the ego claiming to be everything as a way to feel important.

The recognition is more subtle. You're not a separate self that has expanded to include the universe. Rather, what you took to be a separate self is recognized as conceptual structure arising in awareness that has no boundaries.

The universe appears in awareness. The sense of being a separate self also appears in awareness. Neither is what you fundamentally are—awareness is prior to both constructs.

Better: "The boundary between self and universe is conceptual rather than absolute." This is more accurate and less likely to feed ego inflation.

### "Everything Is One"

Another phrase that's technically true but easily misunderstood. It can lead to dismissing important differences, ignoring harm, or avoiding necessary boundaries.

Yes, the apparent separation between things arises within unified field. But the differences are real as differences. You and the tree are not identical even if the boundary between you isn't absolute.

The recognition includes multiplicity, not denies it. There's both unity and diversity, both boundlessness and form, both the field and the distinct expressions within the field.

### "Boundaries Are Illusion"

This misunderstanding can cause real harm. If boundaries are illusion, why respect consent? Why maintain personal limits? Why not violate others' space since separation isn't real?

No. Boundaries are conceptual rather than metaphysical, but they're important concepts. They serve function. They protect. They enable relationship.

The recognition is that boundaries are constructed rather than inherent—but constructed doesn't mean unimportant. Most of civilization depends on constructed boundaries. Honor them while knowing their nature.

### "Space Doesn't Exist"

This is too strong. Space exists as relative framework, as relationship between positions, as useful way to organize experience. What's recognized is that space isn't container awareness exists in, and spatial separation isn't as absolute as it appears.

You still need to navigate space. Distance still matters for practical purposes. Objects still have relative positions. All of this remains functional.

What changes is your relationship to spatial experience—you know it as framework arising in awareness rather than as fundamental reality that awareness is subject to.

## The Depth of the Recognition

Like the recognition of timelessness, recognizing boundlessness has layers:

**Initially**, you understand it conceptually. "Interesting—boundaries might be conceptual." This plants a seed but doesn't transform experience.

**With inquiry**, you begin catching the assumed boundary and looking for it. "Where is this edge I take for granted?" The assumption starts to loosen.

**With glimpses**, you directly recognize boundlessness. For a moment, the sense of being located dissolves. You are the field in which all locations appear. This is striking when it first happens—like suddenly seeing through an optical illusion you've been lost in your entire life.

**With stability**, the recognition becomes familiar. You still function with apparent location, but you know it's appearance in boundless awareness. The grip of spatial separation relaxes.

**With maturity**, even the concept of boundlessness falls away. You're not trying to remember you're boundless or maintain recognition of spaciousness. It's simply obvious. Form appears, including the form of apparently bounded body, and there's no problem with any of it because nothing has ever actually been separate.

This maturation is familiarization, not achievement. The boundlessness was always here. You're just becoming increasingly transparent to what's always been true.

## A Final Paradox

You've read this article from beginning to end—a spatial metaphor. Beginning "here," end "there." Your eyes moved across space, following words arranged spatially on the page or screen.

And yet, the reading happens in awareness that has no location. The beginning and end both appear now in boundless knowing. The spatial arrangement serves communication while appearing in what has no position.

Can you notice that? The words seem to be "out there" on the screen. But the seeing of them, the understanding arising—where is that? Can you find a location for awareness itself?

Look not for answer but for what's looking. The looking has no position. It's not in your head or anywhere else. It's the boundless openness in which head, screen, words, understanding—all appear.

That's what you are.

Not bounded by skin.
Not located in space.
Not separate from what appears.

The boundless awareness that contains all boundaries, includes all locations, is prior to all division into here and there.

You always have been this.
You always will be this.
Though "have been" and "will be" and even "this" are just ways of speaking about what can only be... here.

Everywhere.
Nowhere.
The boundless here that was never actually bound.
