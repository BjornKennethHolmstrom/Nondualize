# Right Now, What's Actually Happening?

> Distinguishing direct experience from story about experience

*The mind is a magnificent story-maker. It doesn't just register what's happening—it immediately interprets, evaluates, compares, and weaves narrative around every experience. This happens so quickly, so automatically, that we rarely notice the difference between raw experience and the story about it. This article invites you to make that distinction—to notice what's actually here before the interpretation begins.*

## The Default Mode: Story First

Most of the time, you're not experiencing life directly. You're experiencing your thoughts ABOUT life.

**Example:**

Someone criticizes your work. What happens?

**The story version (what most people experience):**
- "They think I'm incompetent"
- "I always mess things up"
- "Now everyone will judge me"
- "I should have done better"
- Anxiety, shame, defensiveness

**The direct experience (what's actually happening):**
- Sound waves (their voice)
- Certain words spoken
- Sensation in chest and stomach
- Thoughts arising
- Emotion moving through

**Notice the difference?** The story adds layers of meaning, past references, future projections, self-concept. The direct experience is simpler, more immediate, more workable.

**This inquiry asks:** Can you distinguish between the two?

## The First Distinction: Sensation vs. Interpretation

Let's start with something simple.

**Right now, notice your body.**

**What do you actually feel?**

Not "I'm tired" or "I'm comfortable"—those are interpretations. What are the actual sensations?

**Try this:**
- Scan your body slowly
- Notice specific sensations: pressure, temperature, tingling, tension
- Name them descriptively rather than interpretively
- "Tightness in shoulders" rather than "I'm stressed"
- "Warmth in belly" rather than "I feel good"

**What do you find?**

Most people discover that raw sensation is neutral—it's just energy, movement, pressure, temperature. The judgment about whether it's good or bad comes afterward.

## The Second Distinction: Perception vs. Meaning

Look around the room. What do you see?

**The interpreted version:**
- "That's my favorite chair"
- "The room is messy"
- "Those books need organizing"

**The direct perception:**
- Shapes, colors, light, shadow
- Movement or stillness
- Spatial relationships

**Try this experiment:**
1. Look at an object nearby
2. Notice the shape, color, texture
3. Now notice any thoughts ABOUT the object
4. Can you distinguish between:
   - The visual appearance itself
   - Your thoughts about what it means, whether you like it, what you should do with it?

**The gap between perception and meaning:**

There's a moment—however brief—where the perception registers before meaning is assigned. That moment is direct experience. Everything after is interpretation.

## The Third Distinction: Event vs. Story

Something happens. Then the mind creates a narrative about what it means.

**Example: Your phone rings**

**Direct experience:**
- Sound occurring
- Hand reaching
- Screen appearing
- Visual symbols

**Story layer:**
- "Who could this be?"
- "I hope it's not bad news"
- "I should call them back later"
- "This always happens when I'm busy"

**The event itself is simple.** The story creates complexity, drama, emotional charge.

**Try noticing this today:**

When something happens—anything—can you catch the moment before the story begins?

## The Practice: Staying With What Is

This is harder than it sounds. The mind generates story so automatically, so quickly, that catching it requires real attention.

**Instructions:**

1. **Choose a simple activity** - Making tea, walking, washing hands
2. **Notice the direct experience** - Temperature, movement, sensation, sound
3. **When story arises** (and it will) - Notice: "Ah, thinking"
4. **Return to direct experience** - What's actually happening right now?
5. **Repeat** - This is the practice

**What you'll discover:**

Direct experience is:
- **Simpler** than the story
- **Constantly changing** (the story pretends things are more solid)
- **Already complete** (not lacking anything)
- **Immediately workable** (you can respond to what's actually here)

The story is:
- **Complex** (layered with meaning)
- **Seemingly permanent** ("This is how I always am")
- **Incomplete** ("If only... then I'd be okay")
- **Often overwhelming** (the story about pain is often worse than the pain itself)

## Common Examples

Let's look at how this distinction shows up in daily life:

### Physical Pain

**The story:**
- "This is terrible"
- "It's getting worse"
- "What if it never goes away?"
- "I can't handle this"

**Direct experience:**
- Sensation in specific location
- Quality: sharp, dull, throbbing, burning
- Intensity fluctuating
- Perhaps fear arising as thought and sensation

**What's interesting:** When you stay with the direct experience of pain without the story, it's often more workable. Not that it doesn't hurt—but the suffering (which is the story about the pain) decreases.

### Anxiety

**The story:**
- "Something bad is going to happen"
- "I won't be able to handle it"
- "Everyone will see I'm a fraud"
- "This feeling will never end"

**Direct experience:**
- Tightness in chest or stomach
- Rapid heartbeat
- Quick, shallow breathing
- Thoughts racing
- Energy moving through the body

**The shift:** Anxiety is the story. What's actually here is sensation and thought—both workable when met directly.

### Boredom

**The story:**
- "Nothing interesting is happening"
- "I should be doing something else"
- "This is a waste of time"
- "I need stimulation"

**Direct experience:**
- Body sensation (restlessness, maybe)
- Thoughts about wanting something different
- Perhaps tiredness or energy
- The present moment, exactly as it is

**The discovery:** Boredom is a story about the present moment being insufficient. What's actually here is simpler and less problematic.

## The Deeper Recognition

As you practice distinguishing direct experience from story, you might notice something profound:

**The story is ALSO direct experience.**

The thoughts themselves are arising in awareness. They're not separate from what's happening—they're part of it. The difference is:

**When you're identified with the story:**
- It feels true, solid, important
- You ARE the character in the narrative
- The story seems to describe reality

**When you see it AS story:**
- It's recognized as thought, not truth
- You're the awareness in which the story appears
- The story is seen as interpretation, not reality itself

**This doesn't mean stories are bad or useless.** They're incredibly useful for navigating life. The problem comes from:
- Not recognizing them as stories
- Believing them absolutely
- Getting lost in them
- Mistaking them for what's actually happening

## A Deeper Experiment

**Right now:**

1. **Notice what's actually happening**
   - Sounds, sensations, visual field
   - Thoughts arising and passing
   - Breathing, heartbeat

2. **Notice the story about it**
   - "This exercise is interesting/boring"
   - "I'm doing this right/wrong"
   - "I wonder if this will help"

3. **Notice you're aware of both**
   - The direct experience
   - The story
   - Both arising in the same awareness

**The question:** What are you? The experience? The story? Or the awareness that contains both?

## When Direct Experience Gets Difficult

**Important note:** This inquiry isn't about using "direct experience" to avoid difficult emotions. It's about meeting them more directly.

**If you're experiencing strong emotion:**

**Don't bypass it with:** "It's just sensation, it doesn't matter"

**Instead, meet it directly:**
- What exactly is the sensation?
- Where is it located?
- How does it move or change?
- What thoughts accompany it?

**The difference:**
- Bypassing: Using concepts to create distance
- Direct meeting: Being present TO what's actually here

**Often what's overwhelming is the story ABOUT the emotion plus the resistance to it.** When you meet the direct experience—the actual sensation and energy—it's more workable than you expected.

## Integration: Living From Direct Experience

As this distinction becomes clearer, something shifts:

**In meditation:**
- Less struggle with "doing it right"
- More curiosity about what's actually here
- Stories recognized as stories
- Space for everything to be as it is

**In difficult moments:**
- Emotions less overwhelming
- Problems more workable
- Less catastrophizing
- More present-moment response possible

**In relationships:**
- Hearing what's actually said vs. what you think is meant
- Responding to the person vs. your story about them
- Less reactivity, more clarity

**In daily life:**
- More vivid, immediate
- Less sense of being trapped in your head

- Simple presence becomes available

## Common Questions

### "But don't I need my stories to function?"

Yes! Stories are useful. Planning requires imagining the future. Learning requires remembering the past. The issue isn't having stories—it's:
- Being unconsciously identified with them
- Believing them absolutely
- Missing what's actually here because you're lost in narrative

**The freedom:** Using stories skillfully while knowing they're stories.

### "Isn't focusing on direct experience just another story?"

Clever mind! Yes, in a sense. But there's a difference:
- Most stories take you AWAY from present experience
- This inquiry brings you TOWARD what's actually happening

Eventually, even the distinction between "direct experience" and "story" can be released. But first, making the distinction is helpful.

### "What about positive stories? Should I give those up too?"

This isn't about giving anything up. It's about seeing clearly. 

Positive stories ("I'm capable," "Life is good") are still stories. When you're identified with them, they create:
- Dependency (you need the story to feel okay)
- Fragility (when circumstances challenge the story)
- Separation (from what's actually true right now)

**The freedom:** You can enjoy positive experiences without needing them to mean something about "you."

### "How is this different from mindfulness?"

It's very related! Mindfulness often emphasizes "being present to what is." This inquiry specifically highlights the difference between:
- What is
- The story about what is

Same territory, slightly different emphasis.

## A Daily Practice

**Morning:**

When you first wake up, before checking your phone, notice:
- What's actually here (sensation, breathing, light)
- What stories immediately arise ("I'm tired," "I have so much to do")
- The difference between the two

**Throughout the day:**

Set a reminder (every hour or so) to pause and ask:
- "Right now, what's actually happening?"
- Notice direct experience first
- Then notice any story
- Rest in the distinction

**Evening:**

Review your day:
- When did you get caught in story?
- When were you present to direct experience?
- What difference did it make?

**No judgment needed.** Just noticing.

## The Recognition

At some point—maybe gradually, maybe suddenly—you might recognize:

**The story of "me" is also a story.**

Not that you don't exist. But that what you are isn't the narrative.

The story runs: "I'm a person who thinks, feels, does things, has a past and future, struggles and succeeds."

The direct experience: There's awareness. There's experiencing happening. But no solid, continuous "me" can be found—just story arising in awareness.

**This is profoundly liberating.**

Not because you transcend being human, but because you're no longer trapped in the narrative about being human.
