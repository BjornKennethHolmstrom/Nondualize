# Cause and Effect: The Unbroken Wholeness

> Linear causality as abstraction from unified field

*Everything seems to happen because something else happened. You're reading this because you clicked a link. You clicked the link because you were interested. You were interested because of past experiences. Those experiences happened because of earlier causes. The chain extends backward infinitely and forward infinitely—each effect becoming the cause of the next effect. This causal chain feels like the fundamental structure of reality. Science depends on it. Moral responsibility requires it. Understanding anything means tracing causes. And yet... when you look closely at direct experience, can you actually find separate causes and effects? Or do you find events arising together, simultaneously, interdependently—with the causal story being interpretation imposed afterward? This article examines causality not as science describes it, but as you directly experience it, exploring whether the linear sequence from cause to effect is the nature of reality or a useful fiction.*

## The Conventional View

Let's start with how we normally understand causality.

**Every effect has a cause**. Nothing happens without reason. The universe is deterministic—current state follows necessarily from previous state. Even if quantum randomness exists at microscopic levels, at the level of everyday experience, causality rules.

**Causes precede effects in time**. First the cause happens, then the effect follows. The sequence is absolute. You can't have the effect before the cause. Time's arrow and causality's arrow point the same direction.

**Understanding means finding causes**. To understand why something happened, you trace back to its causes. Why did the glass break? Because it fell. Why did it fall? Because you bumped the table. Why did you bump the table? Because you were distracted. Each "why" is answered by pointing to the prior cause.

**Actions have consequences**. What you do now causes effects in the future. This is basis for responsibility, planning, learning. You're accountable for your actions because your actions cause outcomes. You plan for the future because present actions cause future conditions.

**The chain is linear**. A causes B, B causes C, C causes D. One thing leads to another in orderly sequence. You can map the causal chain, predict effects from causes, manipulate causes to produce desired effects.

This causal framework organizes virtually everything: science, morality, law, technology, personal responsibility, historical understanding. Without causality, the world seems chaotic, meaningless, incomprehensible.

But is causality describing reality as it is? Or is it imposing structure on reality to make it comprehensible?

## The Direct Inquiry

Let's examine causality in immediate experience rather than in conceptual models.

### Can You Find the Cause?

Think of something that just happened—you moved your hand, you heard a sound, you had a thought.

What caused it?

You might have a story: "I decided to move my hand, so my decision caused the movement." But look more closely. Can you find the decision as a separate event that caused the movement? Or is there just the arising of intention and movement together, with you calling one "cause" and the other "effect" afterward?

You might say: "The sound was caused by the door closing." But in direct experience, there's just the sound appearing now. The door closing isn't present now—only memory of it, or inference about it. The causal connection between door-closing and sound-hearing is story, not direct experience.

Try to catch the moment where cause becomes effect. Try to observe the actual transition from one to the other. Can you find it? Or is there always just present experience, with causal explanations being thoughts about experience?

### The Timing Problem

Causality requires temporal sequence: cause first, then effect. But as we explored in Article 5-1, past and future are thoughts appearing now. The "first" and "then" are conceptual, not directly experienced.

When you remember the cause and experience the effect, both are present now—one as memory, one as current sensation. The temporal sequence is inferred, not perceived.

Even in immediate succession—you snap your fingers and hear the sound—can you find two separate moments, or is there one unified event that you conceptually divide into cause (snap) and effect (sound)?

The physicist might say: "The cause objectively precedes the effect, even if you can't perceive the gap." But that's a model, a conceptual framework. In direct experience, there's just arising in the eternal now, with temporal sequence being interpretation.

### The Interdependence Recognition

Buddhism speaks of "dependent origination" or "interdependent arising"—nothing exists independently; everything arises in relation to everything else.

Look at any "effect." Is it really produced by a single "cause," or by countless conditions coming together?

You're reading these words. What's the cause? Your decision to read? But that decision depended on interest, which depended on past experiences, which depended on genetic predispositions, which depended on evolutionary history, which depended on cosmic conditions. The screen displaying these words depends on electricity, technology, economic systems, human knowledge accumulated over millennia.

The "cause" of you reading this moment isn't one thing. It's everything. The entire universe in its current configuration is the "cause," and this moment is the "effect"—except the "effect" is also "cause" of the next moment, so the distinction dissolves.

Everything arises together, mutually conditioning, without clear boundaries between cause and effect. The causal chain isn't a chain at all—it's a web, or better, a unified field where divisions into cause and effect are conceptual conveniences.

### The Simultaneity of Arising

In quantum mechanics, there's a principle that observation affects what's observed. The act of measurement doesn't just reveal pre-existing properties—it participates in creating those properties.

Quantum mechanics presents a serious challenge to naive causality. In phenomena like quantum entanglement, the measurement of one particle instantly affects its partner, regardless of distance, suggesting a deeper, non-local wholeness. The "delayed-choice experiment" even suggests that present observations can influence what appears to have happened in the past.

This doesn't mean "anything goes," but it strongly points toward a universe that is fundamentally interconnected and holistic, where the linear, local cause-effect story is a limited, emergent approximation.

Some interpretations suggest retrocausality—the present affecting the past, or more accurately, the past not being fully determined until the present observation occurs.

This violates common-sense causality where past determines present. It suggests something more like: past and present arise together, mutually determining each other, with the linear sequence being how we organize experience rather than how reality actually unfolds.

Look at your own experience. When you remember something, does the past determine your memory? Or does your present state—your mood, your needs, your current understanding—shape what you remember and how you remember it?

The past and present seem to arise together, each conditioning the other, without clear priority of one over the other.

### The Problem of Free Will

If present is caused by past, and past was caused by earlier past, then everything is determined. There's no room for free will, for choice, for genuine newness.

But if there's free will—if you can genuinely choose—then the present isn't fully determined by the past. Something can arise that isn't purely effect of prior causes.

The free will versus determinism debate assumes causality is fundamental. But what if causality is conceptual framework rather than metaphysical reality?

What if there's just arising—events happening, choices occurring, the universe unfolding—without actual causal chains connecting one moment to the next? The connections we perceive are patterns we recognize, not forces that compel.

Action happens. The sense of choosing happens. Learning from consequences happens. All without requiring that causes in the past mechanistically produce effects in the present.

### The Causal Story After the Fact

Notice how you construct causal explanations after events occur, not before.

Something happens. Then you ask "why?" and construct a story tracing causes. The story feels like you're discovering pre-existing causal connections, but you're actually creating coherence retrospectively.

Notice this in your own mind: when something surprising or negative happens, there is first the raw event, and then a frantic search for a "cause." The mind cannot rest until it has constructed a story: "This happened because..." This story creates a sense of understanding and control, but it is always a retrospective fabrication. The actual, seamless arising of the event remains prior to any "because."

This is why different people explain the same event with different causal stories. The event is the same, but the causal interpretation varies depending on perspective, values, what you're looking for.

The event itself doesn't come with causal labels attached. You add those labels to make sense of what happened, to find patterns, to predict and control.

This doesn't mean causal thinking is useless. It's incredibly useful. But useful and true aren't the same thing.

## The Recognition

When you examine causality directly:

**Causal relationships are conceptual**. You don't directly experience cause producing effect. You experience events and infer causal relationships between them based on patterns, temporal sequence, and conceptual models.

**Everything arises together**. The universe at this moment is a unified whole. Dividing it into separate causes and effects is useful for manipulation and prediction, but the divisions aren't inherent in reality itself.

**Past and present are mutually conditioning**. The past doesn't unilaterally determine the present. The present shapes how the past is understood and remembered. They arise together in the timeless now, each giving meaning to the other.

**Determinism and free will are both incomplete**. The question assumes causality is fundamental. When causality is seen as conceptual framework, the question dissolves. 

The debate between free will and determinism is a conceptual trap. Both assume a self-existing "you" that is either the "source" of free actions or a "pawn" in a causal chain. When the separate self is seen through (Article 5-3) and causality is seen as a pattern rather than a force, the question itself dissolves.

What remains is simply arising: choices happen, actions flow, life unfolds. It is neither determined nor random, but an expression of the whole universe's creative unfolding. You are not a separate chooser, nor a puppet; you are the activity of the whole, manifesting as this particular choosing.

**Responsibility remains**. Actions have consequences, patterns exist, learning happens. None of this requires that causes in the past mechanistically force effects in the present. You can act responsibly, learn from experience, and plan for the future while knowing that causality is useful map rather than territory itself.

**The causal framework serves**. Science, technology, morality, personal growth—all employ causal thinking successfully. The recognition isn't to abandon causal thinking but to know it as tool rather than truth, framework rather than fundamental reality.

This isn't philosophy or physics. It's what's revealed when you look at how causality actually appears in experience versus how you think about it.

## But What About...?

Questioning causality triggers deep resistance because so much depends on it.

### Don't Things Obviously Cause Other Things?

Yes, in relative, conventional sense. The hammer hits the glass, the glass breaks. The cause and effect are obvious.

But look at what "cause" means here. It means: given certain conditions (glass, hammer, sufficient force, specific angle), a certain outcome reliably follows. It's a pattern, a regularity, a predictable relationship.

Does the hammer reaching the glass force the breaking through some invisible causal connection? Or do the conditions simply give rise to breaking as their natural expression?

The difference is subtle but significant. One view sees causality as mechanism—real forces connecting separate events. The other sees causality as description—recognition of patterns in how things unfold.

Both are useful. But direct experience can't verify the mechanism—only the pattern. The causal force itself is never perceived, only inferred.

### Doesn't Science Prove Causality?

Science is incredibly successful at prediction and control through causal models. This success seems to prove causality is real.

But science describes regularities, not ultimate mechanisms. "If X, then Y" is a description of correlation and predictable sequence, not necessarily proof of causal force.

Modern physics actually questions classical causality. Quantum mechanics shows that observation affects outcomes in ways that violate simple cause-effect models. Relativity shows that simultaneity is relative—events that are sequential in one reference frame are simultaneous in another.

Some physicists speak of the universe as a unified four-dimensional block where past, present, and future all exist simultaneously, with the flow of time and causality being artifacts of perspective rather than features of fundamental reality.

Science doesn't require metaphysical causality—it requires predictable patterns. And patterns exist without needing to be produced by prior causes forcing subsequent effects.

### What About Moral Responsibility?

If actions don't cause consequences in some direct, mechanistic way, can people be held responsible?

Yes. Responsibility doesn't require metaphysical causality. It requires that actions have consequences, that people can learn and change, that society can encourage beneficial behavior and discourage harmful behavior.

All of this works whether causality is real mechanism or useful framework. Actions still lead to outcomes. Learning still happens. Consequences still follow patterns.

What changes is the nature of blame and credit. If everything is mutually arising, interdependently conditioned, then there's no ultimate "cause" to punish or reward. But there's still appropriate response—addressing harmful patterns, supporting beneficial ones.

This perspective fosters a justice rooted in healing and restoration rather than pure retribution. If a harmful act arises from a tangled web of conditions (including personal history, societal factors, and immediate circumstances), then the most effective response is to address those conditions—both within the individual and the society—to prevent future harm.

This includes holding a clear, firm boundary against harmful behavior, not because we blame a fundamentally separate "evil agent," but because we are responding to a harmful pattern with the intention of healing the whole system.

Responsibility becomes response-ability—capacity to respond appropriately—rather than metaphysical causation requiring retribution or reward.

### Doesn't This Make Everything Random?

If causes don't determine effects, doesn't that mean anything could happen? Complete chaos?

No. Patterns exist. Regularities are real. The universe is reliable and consistent. None of this requires that metaphysical causality is fundamental.

Think of it this way: the universe unfolds according to its nature. Given certain conditions, certain outcomes naturally follow—not because prior conditions force later conditions, but because that's how reality expresses itself.

It's more like a musical performance than a mechanical process. Each note follows naturally from the musical logic, the composer's vision, the performer's skill—but not because prior notes mechanistically cause subsequent notes. The whole piece is unified, with each part expressing the whole.

Similarly, the universe is unified wholeness. Events arise according to the nature of the whole, with apparent causal sequences being how we parse the unified unfolding into comprehensible parts.

### How Can I Plan for the Future?

If present doesn't cause future in some deterministic way, how can you plan? How can you know that actions now will lead to desired outcomes later?

Patterns. Regularities. The universe is consistent. Actions lead to consequences in predictable ways—not because of metaphysical causality, but because that's how the unified field expresses itself.

You can plan, prepare, act skillfully. All the practical tools of causal thinking remain available. What drops away is the sense that you're manipulating independent causes to force specific effects. Instead, you're participating in the unfolding, working with patterns, responding to what arises.

This often makes action more effective, not less. You're not trying to control a mechanical universe through force. You're dancing with a living reality, responding to its movements, contributing your part to the whole.

## Living From Unbroken Wholeness

What changes when linear causality is seen as framework rather than fundamental reality?

### Reduced Regret and Anxiety

Much regret comes from believing: "If only I had done X differently, Y wouldn't have happened." The past action caused the unwanted outcome, so you're stuck with guilt and self-blame.

But if causality is more like mutual arising than mechanical determination, then the past and present arose together from the whole. Yes, your action was involved. But so was everything else. The entire universe in that moment gave rise to that outcome.

This doesn't eliminate responsibility or learning. You still recognize: "That action led to that outcome; I'll act differently next time." But the crushing weight of being the sole cause dissolves.

Similarly with anxiety about the future. If present doesn't mechanistically determine future, then the future isn't locked in by your current inadequacies or mistakes. Each moment arises fresh from the whole. New possibilities are always available.

### Greater Spontaneity

When you believe you're manipulating causes to produce effects, action becomes effortful. You're trying to control a mechanism through force of will.

When causality is seen as framework, action becomes more spontaneous. You respond to what's here, participate in the unfolding, contribute your part without grasping at outcomes.

This is what's meant by *wu wei* in Taoism—action through non-action, effortless effort. Not forcing causes to produce effects, but allowing action to arise naturally from the situation.

The doing still happens, but without the sense of being a separate cause trying to produce specific effects. Action flows from the whole, through you, as appropriate response to conditions.

### Appreciation for Mystery

Causal thinking creates the illusion that you understand why things happen. You can trace the causes, explain the mechanisms, comprehend the process.

When causality is seen as interpretive framework, mystery returns. You don't actually know why things happen. You recognize patterns, observe regularities, create useful models—but the fundamental nature of arising remains mysterious.

This isn't loss. It's gain. The world becomes more alive, less mechanical, more wondrous. Things happen, life unfolds, consciousness explores itself—and the how and why remain ultimately unknown.

### Compassion Without Blame

When causality is absolute, someone is always to blame. Bad outcomes must have bad causes, which means someone caused the problem, which means they deserve punishment.

But if everything arises mutually from the whole, then while actions and consequences are real, absolute blame doesn't make sense. Yes, this person's actions were involved. But so were their circumstances, their history, the entire web of conditions that included that action.

Compassion becomes easier. Not because you excuse harmful behavior, but because you see it as arising from the totality of conditions rather than from a separate, blame-worthy agent.

You can still address harmful patterns, prevent future harm, hold people accountable—all more effectively when not caught in the blame game that causality-thinking creates.

### Presence to What's Arising

When you're constantly tracking causes and effects, you're living in explanations rather than in immediacy. Every experience is immediately categorized: "This is happening because of that. This will cause that."

When causality is loosened, you can be more present to what's actually arising. This sensation, this thought, this moment—just as it is, without immediately filing it in causal categories.

Life becomes less explained and more experienced. The conceptual overlay thins. The raw is-ness becomes more available.

## Practices for Recognition

These aren't techniques to understand causality conceptually, but ways to notice how causal thinking operates and what's prior to it.

### The Cause Search

Throughout your day, when something happens, try to find the cause.

Something appears—a thought, a sensation, a sound, an action. What caused it?

You'll have a story immediately: "I thought that because..." "The sound happened because..." But can you find the actual cause? Can you observe the causal connection, or only infer it?

Look for the mechanism that connects cause to effect. Try to see the actual forcing, the actual production of effect by cause. Can you find it in direct experience?

This isn't to prove causes don't exist. It's to notice the difference between causal thinking (which happens) and causal observation (which doesn't, quite).

### The Interdependence Reflection

Choose any object or event. Ask: "What are all the causes that led to this?"

At first, you'll find a few obvious causes. But keep going. Each cause had its own causes. And each of those had causes.

Follow the web backward. Include not just immediate causes but background conditions, historical events, cosmic circumstances. The whole universe had to be exactly as it was for this particular thing to arise exactly as it did.

Now recognize: if everything is cause of this thing, and this thing is effect of everything, then the cause-effect distinction breaks down. It's all one unified arising, with the causal labels being convenient fictions for parsing the wholeness.

### The Simultaneity Notice

Try to catch causality in action. Watch something happen that has an obvious cause—drop a pen, speak a word, move your hand.

Can you observe two separate moments—first the cause, then the effect? Or is there just one unified event that you conceptually divide into before and after, cause and effect?

Even in cases where there's clear temporal sequence (you drop the pen, then it hits the floor), notice that both events exist now—one as current sensation, one as immediate memory. The sequence is reconstructed in thought, not perceived directly.

This isn't denying that the pen hit the floor after you dropped it. It's noticing that the temporal sequence and causal connection are interpretations of unified experiencing, not separate moments connected by causal force.

### The Responsibility Investigation

Think of something you did that you regret. Something you blame yourself for because it caused harm.

Feel the weight of that. "I caused that. It's my fault."

Now investigate. Can you find yourself as separate cause? Or is there just: circumstances arising, action arising, outcome arising—all from the totality of conditions that included but wasn't limited to "you"?

This doesn't eliminate responsibility. You were involved, you can learn, you can act differently. But the crushing weight of being the sole cause—of having forced that outcome through your separate agency—that can dissolve.

Feel how different it is to say: "That arose from the whole, through me" versus "I caused that." Both involve taking responsibility, but one is lighter, more honest, more aligned with how things actually occur.

### The Planning Exercise

Make a plan for tomorrow. Notice the causal thinking involved: "If I do X, then Y will happen. Y will cause Z. Therefore I should do X."

Now hold that plan lightly. Recognize it as working with patterns, not controlling causes. You're participating in the arising, contributing your part, responding to conditions—but not forcing the future through manipulation of causes.

Plan skillfully, using all your knowledge of patterns and regularities. But hold the plan as hypothesis rather than mechanism, as dance rather than control, as participation rather than force.

Notice how this changes the quality of planning. Less anxiety about controlling outcomes, more responsiveness to what actually unfolds.

### The Explanation Release

When you find yourself explaining something—"This happened because of that"—pause.

Notice the explanation is a story you're telling after the fact. It's not the event itself. The event just happened; the explanation is interpretation.

Notice how the mind searches for the "because." When something unexpected or negative happens, there's first the raw event, and then an urgent need to construct a causal story. The mind cannot rest until it has found a "cause" that makes sense of what happened. This story creates a sense of understanding and control, but it is always retrospective fabrication.

Don't reject the explanation. It might be useful, accurate, helpful for learning or communication. But recognize it as story about what happened, not description of how it actually happened.

Hold explanations lightly. Use them when they serve, release them when they don't. Let the mystery of arising remain mysterious even while you create useful explanations for practical purposes.

## Integration Challenges

Seeing through causality can create specific difficulties:

### Difficulty Making Decisions

If actions don't cause outcomes in some direct way, how do you choose between options? What's the basis for decision-making?

The challenge here is distinguishing between metaphysical causality and practical patterns. Actions do lead to consequences in regular, predictable ways. You can absolutely use that information to make decisions.

What's different is knowing that the causal connection is framework for organizing information, not fundamental mechanism you're manipulating. You're working with patterns, not controlling causes—but the practical result is the same.

If paralysis in decision-making occurs, it suggests you're treating the recognition as philosophical position ("causality isn't real, so I can't decide") rather than as seeing through a particular way of thinking while maintaining practical function.

### Passive Fatalism

"If everything arises from the whole, then nothing I do matters. Why make any effort?"

This is subtle form of nihilism. Yes, your actions arise from the whole rather than from separate, independent agency. But your actions are still part of the whole. What you do matters precisely because it's part of how the whole unfolds.

**Passive fatalism is a misinterpretation.** Thinking "since I don't cause things, I don't need to act" is like a heart cell deciding "since I'm not the cause of circulation, I'll just stop beating." The heart cell is an expression of the circulatory system; its activity is essential to the whole.

Similarly, your actions are not the isolated "cause" of outcomes, but they are a vital, participatory expression of the whole universe's unfolding. To not act is to fail to participate, which is just as much a part of the pattern as acting skillfully. The point is to participate consciously and responsibly, not to abdicate participation.

The recognition isn't: "I'm not responsible." It's: "Responsibility is distributed through the whole, and I'm an expression of that whole." Your choices, your efforts, your responses—all matter because they're how the universe acts through this particular form.

### Avoidance of Consequences

"It wasn't really my fault—everything arises from the whole."

This is using the recognition to bypass accountability. Yes, your action arose from totality of conditions. But you were the condition through which harm occurred. You can learn, change, respond differently—that's your responsibility.

The recognition doesn't eliminate consequences or accountability. It eliminates the crushing weight of metaphysical blame while maintaining practical responsibility and the need for learning and change.

### Confusion About Learning

If the past doesn't cause the present, how does learning work? How do past experiences affect future behavior?

Patterns. The universe is consistent. Similar conditions tend to give rise to similar outcomes. You learn by recognizing patterns and responding to them.

This happens whether causality is fundamental mechanism or useful framework. The practical process of learning is unchanged. What changes is understanding that learning works through pattern recognition rather than through past mechanistically determining future.

## Common Misunderstandings

### "Causality Is Illusion"

Too strong. Causality is useful framework, reliable pattern-recognition, successful model for prediction. It's just not fundamental metaphysical mechanism.

Better: "Causality is interpretive framework that works well for practical purposes while not describing ultimate nature of reality."

### "Nothing Causes Anything"

Misleading. Events arise in relationship to other events. Actions lead to outcomes. The patterns are real.

What's recognized is that the relationship between events isn't mechanical force from cause to effect, but unified arising where we label one aspect "cause" and another "effect" for practical purposes.

### "The Past Doesn't Matter"

Wrong. The past matters immensely—as pattern, as condition, as aspect of the present. What's recognized is that past doesn't unilaterally determine present through causal force.

The past and present arise together, mutually conditioning. Both matter. The relationship between them is more subtle than simple causation.

### "Free Will Is Proven"

Also wrong. The recognition doesn't prove free will exists. It shows that both determinism and free will assume causality is fundamental. When causality is seen as framework, the question shifts.

The debate between free will and determinism is a conceptual trap. Both assume a self-existing "you" that is either the "source" of free actions or a "pawn" in a causal chain. When causality is seen as pattern rather than force, this either/or question dissolves.

There's arising. Choices happen. Actions occur. It is neither determined nor random, but an expression of the whole universe's creative unfolding. Whether to call this "free will" or "determinism" becomes less important than recognizing the actual nature of the arising itself.

## The Depth of the Recognition

Like other recognitions, this unfolds in layers:

**Conceptually**, you understand that causality might be framework rather than reality. Interesting intellectual point.

**With inquiry**, you notice that you can't actually observe causal connections—only events and inferred relationships. The conceptual starts affecting how you see experience.

**With glimpses**, the causal framework temporarily dissolves. Everything is just arising, just happening, without clear division into causes and effects. This is striking—like seeing a magic trick explained and recognizing the illusion.

**With stability**, you live from recognition that causality is useful tool rather than fundamental truth. You use causal thinking for practical purposes while knowing what you're doing. The grip of determinism loosens. Responsibility becomes lighter but not absent.

**With maturity**, even talking about "causality as framework" falls away. Things just happen. Actions occur. Consequences follow patterns. Learning continues. All without needing to believe in or disbelieve in causality. It's simply obvious that the causal story is story, and reality is prior to the story.

This is familiarization, not achievement. The unified wholeness was always how things were. You're just becoming transparent to what was always true.

## A Final Paradox

You're at the end of this article because you read through it. The reading caused you to reach this point.

Or did it?

Was there reading (cause) and then reaching the end (effect)? Or was there just the unified experience of reading-reaching-the-end, which you parse into temporal sequence and causal relationship?

The article "caused" understanding to arise—or did it? Understanding arose in conditions that included the article, but also your readiness, your past experiences, your current context, the entire universe arranged exactly as it is.

Can you notice how the causal explanation feels compelling but isn't actually observed? How there's just experience arising, with the causal story being interpretation added after?

That's the recognition.

Not that causes and effects don't exist.
Not that past doesn't influence present.
Not that actions don't have consequences.

But that the relationship between events is unified wholeness parsed into conceptual divisions, not separate entities connected by invisible causal forces.

All of reality arises together.
Each moment expresses the whole.
Causality is the story we tell about the unified arising.

And knowing this—knowing causality as story rather than structure—frees you to work with patterns skillfully while remaining present to the mystery of each arising moment.
